Life · Personal growth · Originality

Uniqueness Is
a Combination

I do not think we become original by starting from nothing. We become distinctive by combining what we have learned, experienced, felt, and come to value, then using that mix to meet the needs in front of us.

Almost every new idea has a past behind it. We find something useful, build on it, and change it as we understand more or as life asks something different of us. The starting points may be shared; the combination and the way we use it can be our own.

Shared foundations, different expression

Think about language. We all begin with alphabets, grammar, nouns, and other common building blocks. Those foundations do not make every conversation the same. People use them to express different memories, questions, emotions, and ways of seeing the world.

Ideas work similarly. An invention can be genuinely new and still depend on earlier concepts, discoveries, and tools. Building on what exists does not make the result less valuable. Often, the important work is seeing what can be connected, improved, or used in a new context.

Borrowing is a beginning

We borrow ideas as starting points, not instructions to copy an entire solution. The endpoint changes because people have different circumstances, constraints, goals, and priorities. Even one person's needs can change from year to year.

So a useful idea needs interpretation. What should stay? What no longer fits? What is missing for this person, team, or moment? The answers turn a borrowed starting point into something adapted to real needs. Giving credit to the sources we build on is part of that process; so is being clear about what we changed and why.

The combination is the difference

Our mix of interests and skills can also make our contribution distinct. Someone who can build a product, understand how it is sold, and think about the psychology of the people using it may notice a different problem than someone approaching it from only one angle.

I value having a working understanding of many areas, perhaps enough to ask better questions, communicate across disciplines, and recognize useful connections. A rough 10–30% across a range of fields can be a helpful aspiration for breadth, not a universal measure of competence or a substitute for deep expertise. Some work calls for specialists; broader knowledge helps us know when to involve them and how their work connects to the whole.

Keep revising the mix

There is no final combination that defines us forever. New experiences change what we notice; new responsibilities change what matters; new constraints change which ideas are useful. A strength that served one chapter may need to be paired with a different skill in the next.

For me, originality is not a contest to have no influences. It is the practice of learning from what came before, combining it with what I know now, and reshaping it for the problem I actually want to solve. The ingredients may be familiar. The recipe keeps changing because the person, the purpose, and the moment keep changing.
