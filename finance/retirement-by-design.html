Retirement
by Design

My retirement plan is not to stop working. It is to gain more control over what I work on, when I work, and what each hour is worth.

I want to make that shift gradually, while keeping my ability to earn and build useful things. Financial independence, for me, is less a finish line than the freedom to choose.

Two numbers, two different jobs

My personal “enough” number is ₹20 crore. It is a planning marker: a point at which I expect to have meaningful options, not a promise that one amount will fund every life or every future.

My lifetime ambition is ₹100 crore or more. That is a much larger wealth-building target, not the amount I need in order to feel allowed to change how I work. I do not want an ambitious number to keep moving the definition of enough forever.

Both figures need context. Spending, inflation, taxes, investment returns, family responsibilities, and where I live all affect what a portfolio can support. I will need to revisit the assumptions as my life changes; the numbers are not a withdrawal formula or a guarantee.

Reduce the hours, not the usefulness

Using age 30 as my eight-hour baseline, I plan to reduce my salaried workday by 30 minutes each year until age 40. That would bring it to three hours a day by age 40. These are targets to adjust as income and responsibilities allow, not a fixed schedule or a reason to leave work altogether.

Over time, I want more of my work to come from consulting and selling practical playbooks. The bet is that deeper expertise and useful products can let me earn more per hour, while giving me greater choice over the hours themselves. That is a goal to test, not an assumption that consulting or product sales will automatically replace a salary.

Use geography thoughtfully

I am interested in using purchasing-power differences to live well in lower-cost countries while earning and investing across borders. For me, the idea is to make income, spending, and investments work across places with different costs and opportunities, rather than tying every part of my life to one location.

Part of the plan How purchasing power may help What I need to consider
Earn Build expertise and seek consulting work or clients in markets where that work is valued, earning in stronger currencies such as the USD, EUR, or Gulf/Islamic-state dinars. Rates vary by role and client; taxes, work authorization, and reliable demand still matter.
Spend Choose lower-cost places where my income may support a higher standard of living, such as spending in Vietnamese dong or Indian rupees. Local prices, healthcare, residency rules, and currency changes can alter the comparison.
Invest Hold a globally considered portfolio instead of limiting investments to my place of residence, with a larger allocation toward BRICS and other developing-GDP economies. Market risk, currency exposure, taxes, fees, and concentration need their own review.

That flexibility comes with real considerations: currency movements, taxes, healthcare, residency rules, and the risks of concentrating too much of my life or portfolio in one country. Those details need qualified, current advice; geography is not a shortcut around them.

Stay connected to India's growth

I want a meaningful part of my economic life to support India's growth through investment, employment, and the money I spend there. I think about those connections in three distinct ways:

Connection How it fits my plan Potential contribution
Developed economies Seek work and consulting opportunities where my skills can create value, and use them for stability — storing value in a global currency like the USD and registering businesses in hubs such as Singapore. Income, experience, and a stable base that can support my wider financial plan.
Developing economies Consider investments in growing markets with strong growth potential, including India, based on risk and valuation. Capital for businesses and markets, with no guarantee of returns or specific outcomes.
Spending abroad Spend time in lower-currency-value countries with high-speed internet and strong tech exposure, such as Vietnam. A lower cost of living alongside good infrastructure, stretching the same income further.

I am also considering OCI status as part of a life that may span countries. It is a personal identity and residency decision, not simply an investment tactic.

OCI status does not include the right to vote in Indian elections. I understand that economic participation is not a substitute for political representation, and I would need to be comfortable with that trade-off rather than gloss over it.

Progress is more than a portfolio value

I will judge this plan by whether I am building durable earning power, saving and investing consistently, and gaining real choice over my time. If the consulting or playbook business does not work as expected, I can adjust the pace instead of pretending the original timeline is guaranteed.

This is my evolving plan, not a prescription for another investor. The useful question is not only “How much is enough?” It is also “What kind of work and life am I trying to make possible along the way?”
