Dishant Raut · Personal finance · Investing philosophy

Freedom Investing
vs. Active Investing

I want my investments to build wealth in the background, not become another job.

For me, freedom investing means spending my attention where I can create the most value: building useful skills, doing good work, or growing a business. Then I invest the income I create in a way that can compound over decades and support the next generation.

My time has a better use

If I am a surgeon, improving my surgical skill can increase the value of every hour I work. The same is true for a software professional learning and applying valuable skills. I would rather put time into that work than spend every day watching a trading terminal for short-term profits.

That is not a claim that nobody can trade successfully. It is a choice about where I want to spend my time, and what I believe is a better use of it.

My portfolio is not my paycheck

I see my portfolio as a way to distribute income and build wealth, not as a tool I need to trade for income. My goal is long-term ownership and compounding, not trying to generate profits every day, week, or month.

I prefer broad indexes to picking individual stocks or adding actively managed equity funds. My portfolio framework already gives me exposure through different index blocks. Adding more funds can duplicate holdings I already own, so I would rather keep the structure simple and pay attention to costs.

I do use a money-market mutual fund for my emergency fund and dry powder. That serves a different purpose from the long-term equity part of my portfolio.

Buy with a long horizon

I follow the market, but I do not want to follow every tick. When an index looks low to me, often around a 52-week low, I may add to my holdings. I am not claiming to identify the exact bottom. I am investing with a horizon measured in decades and expect to keep accumulating through market declines when I can.

I review periodically rather than daily: around quarterly results, the Union Budget, Diwali, and when a significant event gives me a reason to reassess. Those are opportunities to review my plan, not automatic instructions to trade.

Some parts need more attention

My long-term index holdings are different from thematic or sector positions. I may buy a sector when I believe its prospects are not fully reflected in its price, then sell or reduce that position if the theme loses momentum or my view changes. For example, attention and capital can move from one popular sector to another as new companies and technologies emerge.

I may also reassess exposure when major market or geopolitical events materially change the risks I see. These choices are occasional exceptions, not a plan to predict every turn. Black-swan events are, by definition, difficult to foresee.

Freedom is part of the return

A long-term return matters, but so does the life I live while the portfolio is growing. Less screen-watching and less market noise give me more freedom over my time and where I spend it. I can be present outside work instead of treating every market move as a call to action.

For a broad Indian equity universe such as the Nifty 500, my personal expectation is around 10-12% average annual returns over a five-to-ten-year holding period. That is an expectation, not a guarantee or a reliable forecast. Markets can fall, stay down, or perform differently from what I expect. More important to my plan is continuing to invest through the ups and downs, letting the portfolio compound over my lifetime, and eventually passing it on.

A personal approach, not a prescription

Freedom investing is what fits my priorities: earn through my skills and business, keep my long-term investing broad and relatively simple, add when valuations look attractive, and avoid turning the market into my full-time occupation. The right approach depends on each person's goals, time horizon, responsibilities, and comfort with risk.
