Steps Toward
Financial Freedom

Financial freedom is not a single number or a finish line everyone needs to reach. I think of it as gaining more room to handle uncertainty, make choices, and shape how I spend my time.

Two useful ideas help me think about that journey: the foundations of financial literacy, and the different degrees of choice that financial resources can provide. The foundations are practical building blocks; the levels are a way to describe progress, not a rigid sequence.

Build the financial foundations

  1. Earn a regular income. Income from work, a business, or other sources gives a plan something to build on. Skills, employability, and a realistic view of expenses matter alongside the amount earned.
  2. Start to save and save for emergencies. Set aside an amount you can sustain, even if it starts small. Build accessible emergency savings over time so an unexpected expense is less likely to become expensive debt.
  3. Set financial goals. Decide what you are saving and investing for, and give each goal a timeframe so you can prioritize the next steps.
  4. Understand your net worth and prepare a financial budget. Track income and spending, compare assets with debts, and use that starting point to make a workable budget.
  5. Manage risk. Identify setbacks that could derail your finances and decide which you can absorb, reduce, or transfer. The right protections depend on your responsibilities and circumstances.
  6. Treat insurance as a parachute. Appropriate health, life, disability, or property cover can protect against risks that would be difficult to carry yourself. The types and amount of coverage vary by person.
  7. Manage debt if you have it. Understand each debt's cost, terms, and purpose. Prioritize costly debt while accounting for essential needs; if you have no debt, there is nothing to manage here.
  8. Consider buying a home only if it fits. Home ownership is optional, not a required step toward financial freedom. Compare the full costs, flexibility, location, and time horizon with renting and other priorities.
  9. Invest for your goals and time horizon. Learn how diversification, fees, taxes, and volatility affect a plan. Investments can lose value, so keep money needed soon in forms appropriate for that timeframe.

Seven levels of financial freedom

Money becomes less stressful as you move up the levels. Financial freedom is a journey, not a jump; move up one level at a time.

Level Description
1. Clarity You know where you are financially and what your goals are.
2. Self-sufficiency You can cover your own expenses without outside support.
3. Breathing room You are no longer living paycheck-to-paycheck.
4. Stability You have no bad debt and have a fully funded emergency fund.
5. Flexibility You could take 2 years off of work if needed.
6. Financially independent You can live off of your assets indefinitely.
7. Abundant wealth You have more money than you'll ever need.

Use the framework, not someone else's finish line

The amount needed for stability or independence depends on spending, location, health, family responsibilities, taxes, inflation, and risk. Even a fully funded emergency reserve or a portfolio intended to support spending cannot remove every uncertainty.

I find it more useful to ask what choice I want money to make possible next. That might be handling an emergency without panic, changing work, taking time off, or eventually relying less on earned income. Home ownership, a particular portfolio size, and abundant wealth are not prerequisites for a meaningful degree of freedom.

This is a framework for thinking, not individualized financial advice. The right sequence and trade-offs depend on a person's circumstances and goals.
