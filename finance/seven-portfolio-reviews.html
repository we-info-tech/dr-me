Why I Review My Portfolio
At Least Seven Times a Year

I want to stay deliberate about my portfolio without letting the market occupy my attention every day.

As a freedom investor, I do not want to keep checking prices or reacting to every headline. I use a handful of planned review points instead. They remind me to look at the portfolio, evaluate what has changed, and decide calmly whether any action is needed.

Seven planned review points

My usual rhythm is built around quarterly company results, Budget announcements, and Diwali Muhurat. I count these as review windows, not mandatory trades. An interim Budget is only presented in some years, so the exact calendar can vary.

Review point Why I check in What I might decide
Quarter 1 results Take stock after the first quarterly result period. Hold, add, trim, or rebalance only if my review supports it.
Quarter 2 results Check whether market conditions or my portfolio mix have changed. Compare my holdings and their weights; no change is also a valid outcome.
Quarter 3 results Review the portfolio as the financial year progresses. Consider adding on a dip or rebalancing if the evidence and my plan support it.
Quarter 4 results Make an end-of-year review of holdings, gains, and allocation. Keep holding or adjust exposure when the metrics and portfolio need justify it.
Interim Budget When an interim Budget is presented, consider whether the policy direction changes my outlook. Review rather than trade on the announcement alone.
Final Budget Check whether fiscal policy or spending priorities materially affect my view. Reassess only the holdings or exposures relevant to the change.
Diwali Muhurat Use the occasion as another intentional portfolio check-in. I may act if the setup is in my favour; the occasion itself is not a reason to buy.

What I look at before acting

I review unrealised gains or losses for each holding, its recent highs and lows, and where its price sits relative to the 200-day moving average. I also consider the portfolio's weights and whether uncertainty has changed enough to justify rebalancing.

These are inputs, not automatic instructions. A holding trading near a high, a loss on paper, or a move around the 200-day average does not by itself tell me to buy or sell. I look at the full picture and evaluate the relevant metrics before deciding.

Reviewing is not the same as trading

At a review, I might add to an index position during a dip, trim or sell when a holding looks high against my measures, rebalance for uncertainty, or simply keep holding. I do not place a trade just to meet a count. If nothing needs to change, the review has still done its job: it confirms that I can leave the plan alone.

When I do trade, I consider the decision carefully rather than acting randomly. This limited rhythm helps me stay engaged with my portfolio without turning it into a daily occupation.

Events can add an extra review

Seven is my planned minimum rhythm, not a ceiling. An election result, a change of government, a significant policy shift, geopolitical tension, war, or a change in tariff rates can prompt me to check whether my assumptions or risks have changed. Those events are reasons to review, not automatic signals to sell.

Protecting my attention is part of the plan

My investments are meant to support long-term wealth while I focus on my work, skills, business, and life outside the market. I choose a few moments to look closely, make a considered change when it is warranted, and otherwise let the portfolio do its work. That is how I try to balance staying informed with staying free from constant market noise.

This is my personal review rhythm, not a recommendation or a promise of results. Other investors may need a different schedule based on their goals, holdings, responsibilities, and comfort with risk.
